\chapter{Introduction}
The study how international economic integration affects stability in a country remains one of the central question in development economics. A vast literature (\parencite{dixcarneiro2023globalization, goldberg2007distributional}) looks at the effects of trade liberalization on growth and welfare in advanced economies, yet evidence for developing countries remains limited. In these settings, deep structural inequalities, weaker institutions, and a high reliance on primary commodities may shape very different transmission channels. In these contexts, trade represents an important channel through which external shocks influences local incomes and political stability. \parencite{united2011review}.
The existing empirical literature has mostly focused on commodity-price shocks as the principal external driver of conflict dynamics, like the studies of \textcite{dube2013commodity} and \textcite{bruckner2010international}. They show that international price changes can influence the likelihood of civil conflict, although the direction of the effect depends on whether a country is an exporter or importer of the resource, as well as on the quality of local institutions and the labor intensity of the commodity. Parallel studies investigate how wars and instability disrupt trade and integration, thus the reverse relationship \parencite{rauschendorfer2022trade}. Little evidence exists on how trade shocks themselves, rather than resource or conflict shocks, affect the probability of conflicts. \\
This analysiss attempts to be a first step in studying how exposure to trade influences
subnational conflicts in Nigeria. Unlike previous studies that analyse price fluctuations or cross country variation in trade openness, the present analysis is based on data on port activity, maritime connectivity, and local socio-economic characteristics to measure how the diffusion of trade shocks across space affects the intensity of local conflict events. The aim is to move the focus from aggregate to micro variation, where the mechanisms can be observed more directly.\\

The effect of trade on conflict is theoretically mixed. As stated by \textcite{martin2008civil}, that develop a theoretical framework in which trade openness can have different role in conflict depending on the relative strength of two opposing mechanisms: deterrence, which increases the cost of war through interdependence, and insurance, which reduces mutual dependence and can make conflict more tolerable. Empirical evidence supports both sides of this ambiguity. \textcite{berman2015external}, on which this study is based,, shows that positive external income shocks can reduce conflict because it could improve economic opportunities and raise the opportunity cost of violence. 
Conversely, trade may increase inequality or shift incentives, as discussed by \textcite{amodio2024trade}. This means that the net effect of trade depends on local economic structure, the distribution of gains from trade, and the capacity of institutions to manage distributional tensions.
Other mechanisms can influence this relationship that can influence the final effect of trade, such the type of goods traded and the local institutions, as shown by \textcite{dube2013commodity} and \textcite{berman2017mine}.
In unequal economies, external shocks can therefore operate in two opposite directions: they may stabilize societies by smoothing consumption or destabilize it when benefits are accumulated by elites. We need spatial data to capure how trade exposure varies, and so, to identify  which of these forces dominates. In this context, our analysis contributes by focusing on a distinct empirical channel: variations in maritime trade connectivity across Nigerian coastal regions. By examining localized exposure to global trade, the study explores how integration through ports may either stabilize or destabilize conflict dynamics, depending on local economic conditions, distributional patterns, and institutional capacity.\\

Nigeria has different characteristic that made it a compelling candidate for examining these dynamics, as it is a coastal, rich in natural resources, and regionally fragmented country whose economy depends on external trade. Its geography combines well connected port areas with vast inland regions only loosely integrated into national and global markets. These heterogeneities make the foundation that we need for assessing how trade liberalization could influences conflict through income shocks. 
Moreover, Nigeria exhibits other characterists useful for the study such as marked disparities between East and West regions, ethnic fragmentation, and a recurrent pattern of localized violence, which together amplify the potential for uneven economic transmission. Those channell will be disussed on chapter 3 and 4. 
In particular, Nigerian coastal regions host both mayor commercial ports and resource rich areas, wich is a naturl environment to develop exposure to trade and dependence on extraxting rents.  The Delta Niger region, for instance, has been characterized for long by violence linked by oil extranction and local grivience \parencite{obi2010oil}. At the same time Nigeria's conflict intensity and spatial heterogeneity, as reported in te study of \textcite{bertoni2019education} make it a suistable context to study both the rapacity and opportunity cost channels through which trade may affect conflict dynamics.  
In general, coastal regions benefit from access to maritime infrastructure, while interior districts remain more isolated and vulnerable to economic exclusion. Understanding whether trade openness mitigates or exacerbates such disparities is crucial for interpreting the political economy of globalization in Africa.\\

To capture these mechanisms empirically, we developed a spatial trade exposure index. It combines three components that can link global trade dynamics to local geography: (i) trade shocks at the port level, measured as annual changes in port-level Container Port Throughput (CPT) from UNCTAD; (ii) connectivity of the port to other international one, proxied by the Liner Shipping Connectivity Index (LSCI) from UNCTAD, which is used to quantify each port’s integration into global liner networks; (iii) geographical proximity, expressed as the inverse of the minimum distance between each second level administrative unit (ADM2) and its nearest port. \\

In this specification, smaller distances and higher connectivity imply greater exposure to international trade shocks. The resulting index reflects the idea that trade shocks propagate unequally across space, because it depends on the local access to maritime infrastructure and the integration into global shipping networks \parencite{berman2015external, dube2013commodity}.
This structure ensures that only areas which are geographically and economically close to ports experience significant variation in exposure when trade conditions change.
We constructed the index analysing the interaction between time varying national portual trade shocks (represented by $CPT \times LSCI$) with fixed spatial heterogeneity (that is the inverse distance), constructed this was the index respect the validation request by \textcite{goldsmith2020bartik}. The idea beyond is to captures how fluctuations in global maritime activity translate into differential local effects across Nigeria’s territory in line with the exposure measures used in recent empirical work on trade and conflict \parencite{berman2015external}. \\
A higher value of the index indicates that a district is more exposed to international trade shocks transmitted through Nigeria’s maritime network. This exposure can be interpreted economically as a measure of both opportunity and risk: regions that are closer to more connected ports are more affected by changes in global shipping activity. In peripheral areas, this integration into trade networks could raise the opportunity cost of violence, fostering stability \parencite{berman2015external}. At the same time there could be the opposite mechanism in areas that present more resources. In these regions, trade may amplify competition over rents or reinforce existing inequalities, potentially generating new sources of tension \parencite{dube2013commodity, michalopoulos2013pre}. The spatial variation represented by the proximity to ports, is a key measurement for identifying heterogeneous effects across Nigeria’s territory, because coastal and port-proximate districts are likely to react differently from remote inland areas. 
The index aim to provide a lens through which we can examine how globalization’s benefits and risks are transmitted unequally within a country allowing us to investigate whether the access to international trade serves as a stabilizing force by increasing local opportunities, or as a destabilizing factor when economic gains are concentrated and unequally shared.\\

Building on this framework, we fist linked the constructed exposure index to subnational conflict data from ACLED, focusing on consumption related events that best reflect the opportunity-cost mechanism of conflict dure the 2009–2019 period. Then, we estimated a series of fixed-effects models aiming to isolate the effect of trade exposure from time invariant and spatial characteristics and finally, we explore heterogeneity across regions, sectors, and environmental conditions, examining how local structures mediate the impact of trade integration on stability.\\

Overall, our findings show that greater exposure to international trade is associated with a reduction in conflict, particularly in less developed and more isolated districts, but the stabilizing effect weakens or is reversed in regions that are more developed and are richer in terms of resources, suggesting that the benefits of trade depend largely on how its gains are distributed. These findings contribute to the debate on the political economy of globalization, showing that trade can act as a force for stability when embedded in inclusive local development.
The contribution of this study is double.
First, it aim to provide a sub-national evaluation of trade shocks and conflict in Nigeria, integrating spatially detailed datasets on trade flows, port infrastructure, connectivity, conflict events, and environmental resources. This perspective allows the analysis to move beyond national averages and uncover within country heterogeneity in the economic and social consequences of trade.
Second, the results reveal that the impact of trade liberalization is highly heterogeneous. Exposure to maritime trade tends to reduce conflict incidence less developed districts, but can reinforce instability in wealthier or rent-dependent areas, where new rents or inequalities emerge. This pattern highlights the central role of pre-existing economic and spatial inequalities in mediating the stabilizing effects of globalization.
Beyond its empirical contribution, the thesis also seeks to build a conceptual bridge between two strands of literature that have largely evolved in parallel: studies of external income shocks and conflict \parencite{berman2015external, amodio2024trade} and analyses of spatial exposure to global markets inspired by gravity models \parencite{anderson2003gravity}. By linking these frameworks through a data-driven, geography-based measure of trade integration, the study provides new evidence on how international linkages permeate domestic political economies.
