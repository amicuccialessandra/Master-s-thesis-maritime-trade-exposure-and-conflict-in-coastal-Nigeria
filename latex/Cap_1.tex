\chapter{Introduction}
The study how international economic integration affects stability in a country remains one of the central analysis in development economics. A large body of work looks at the effects of trade liberalization on growth and welfare in advanced economies, yet evidence for developing countries remains limited. In these settings, deep structural inequalities, weaker institutions, and a high reliance on primary commodities may shape very different transmission channels. In these contexts, trade represents an important channel through which external shocks influences local incomes and political stability. \parencite{united2011review}.
The existing empirical literature has mostly focused on commodity-price shocks as the principal external driver of conflict dynamics. In this branch of the literature, we can find studies like \textcite{dube2013commodity} and \textcite{bruckner2010international} showing that international price changes can influence the likelihood of civil conflict, although the direction of the effect depends on whether a country is an exporter or importer of the resource, as well as on the quality of local institutions. Parallel studies investigate how wars and instability disrupt trade and integration, thus the reverse relationship \parencite{martin2008civil}. Little evidence exists on how trade shocks themselves, rather than resource or conflict shocks, affect the probability of conflicts. \\
This analysiss attempts to be a first step in studying how exposure to trade influences
subnational conflicts in Nigeria. Unlike previous studies that analyse price fluctuations or cross country variation in trade openness, the present analysis is based on data on port activity, maritime connectivity, and local socio-economic characteristics to measure how the diffusion of trade shocks across space affects the intensity of local conflict events. The aim is to move the focus from aggregate to micro variation, where the mechanisms can be observed more directly.\\

The effect of trade on conflict is theoretically mixed. Our research follows \textcite{berman2015external}, they find that positive external income shocks can reduce conflict because it could improve economic opportunities and raise the opportunity cost of violence. At the same time, trade may increase inequality or shift incentives, as discussed by \textcite{amodio2024trade}. This means that the net effect depends on local economic structure, the distribution of gains from trade, and the capacity of institutions to manage distributional tensions.
In unequal economies, external shocks can therefore operate in two opposite directions: they may stabilize societies by expanding livelihoods and smoothing consumption or destabilize them when benefits are accumulated by elites. We need spatial data to capure how trade exposure varies, and so, to identify  which of these forces dominates. \\

Nigeria has different characteristic that made it a compelling candidate for examining these dynamics, as it is a coastal, rich in natural resources, and regionally fragmented country whose economy depends on external trade. Its geography combines well connected port areas with vast inland regions only loosely integrated into national and global markets. These heterogeneities make the foundation that we need for assessing how trade liberalization could influences conflict through income shocks.
Moreover, Nigeria exhibits other characterists useful for the study such as marked disparities between East and West regions, ethnic fragmentation, and a recurrent pattern of localized violence, which together amplify the potential for uneven economic transmission. Coastal regions benefit from access to maritime infrastructure, while interior districts remain more isolated and vulnerable to economic exclusion. Understanding whether trade openness mitigates or exacerbates such disparities is crucial for interpreting the political economy of globalization in Africa.\\
To capture these mechanisms empirically, we developed a spatial trade exposure index. It combines three components that can link global trade dynamics to local geography:

1. Trade shocks, measured as annual changes in port-level Container Port Throughput (CPT) from UNCTAD;\\
2. Connectivity of the port to other international one, proxied by the Liner Shipping Connectivity Index (LSCI) from UNCTAD, which is used to quantify each port’s integration into global liner networks; \\
3. Proximity, expressed as the inverse of the minimum distance between each second level administrative unit (ADM2) and its nearest port. \\

In this way smaller distances and higher connectivity imply greater exposure to international trade shocks. This structure ensures that only areas geographically and economically close to ports experience significant variation in exposure when global trade conditions change.
We interacted time varying national portual trade shocks $(CPT \times LSCI)$ with fixed spatial heterogeneity (inverse distance), constructed this was the index respect the validation request by \textcite{goldsmith2020bartik}. It captures how fluctuations in global maritime activity translate into differential local effects across Nigeria’s territory. \\

The contribution of this study is double.
First, it provides a sub-national evaluation of trade shocks and conflict in Nigeria, integrating spatially detailed datasets on trade flows, port infrastructure, connectivity, conflict events, and environmental resources. This perspective allows the analysis to move beyond national averages and uncover within country heterogeneity in the economic and social consequences of trade.
Second, the results reveal that the impact of trade liberalization is highly heterogeneous. Exposure to maritime trade tends to reduce conflict incidence less developed districts, but can reinforce instability in wealthier or rent-dependent areas, where new rents or inequalities emerge. This pattern highlights the central role of pre-existing economic and spatial inequalities in mediating the peace-promoting effects of globalization.
Beyond its empirical contribution, the thesis also offers a conceptual bridge between two strands of literature that have largely evolved in parallel: studies of external income shocks and conflict \parencite{berman2015external, amodio2024trade} and analyses of spatial exposure to global markets inspired by gravity models \parencite{anderson2003gravity}. By linking these frameworks through a data-driven, geography-based measure of trade integration, the study provides new evidence on how international linkages permeate domestic political economies.

The remain of the thesis is organized as follows.Chapter 2 describes the data sources and the construction of key variables. Chapter 3 presents the conceptual framework and empirical strategy. Chapter 4 reports the main empirical results and chapter 5 concludes by summarizing the main findings.
